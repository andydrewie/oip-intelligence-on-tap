5. The problem I would investigate before anything else

[p-8343](/archive/source.html#p-8343)

There’s a commercial paradox in our design.

[p-8387](/archive/source.html#p-8387)

We originally proposed that $106.40 of consumption could be rounded down to $100. That’s a $6.40 discount.

[p-8494](/archive/source.html#p-8494)

Then we added additional credit privileges for loyal subscribers.

[p-8560](/archive/source.html#p-8560)

And now we’ve got potential usage rollover and compute rewards.

[p-8624](/archive/source.html#p-8624)

We’re creating mechanisms that may encourage more spending, while simultaneously reducing the amount of revenue collected per unit of consumption.

[p-8771](/archive/source.html#p-8771)

That could be good business if it increases retention, consumption, and lifetime gross profit enough to offset the discounts and costs. Or it could be economically destructive.

[p-8948](/archive/source.html#p-8948)

I’d want to investigate that before launching anything.

[p-9004](/archive/source.html#p-9004)

In particular, the tests should distinguish:

[p-9049](/archive/source.html#p-9049)

Revenue expansion from new consumption.

[p-9089](/archive/source.html#p-9089)

Revenue lost from customers downgrading or receiving discounts.

[p-9153](/archive/source.html#p-9153)

Additional infrastructure and inference costs.

[p-9200](/archive/source.html#p-9200)

Payment defaults and the cost of administering credit.

[p-9255](/archive/source.html#p-9255)

Retention improvements attributable to loyalty benefits.

[p-9312](/archive/source.html#p-9312)

And I’d run the same comparisons against a conventional $50 tier. Without actual consumption distributions and serving costs, we don’t know which architecture is economically superior.

[p-9497](/archive/source.html#p-9497)

The objective isn’t merely to collect more revenue. It’s to create a durable, mutually beneficial commercial relationship.
